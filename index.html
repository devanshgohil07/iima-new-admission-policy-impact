How much CAT does your marksheet buy?

IIMA changed how it shortlists for the 2027–29 batch. Your CAT result used to enter the formula as a raw score. Now it enters as a percentile. That one swap changes who gets called. Put two candidates in below and this works out exactly how much CAT one of them has to make up for the gap in their marksheet — under the new rules and the old ones, side by side.

Both sets of rules rank candidates on one composite score, split the same way: 35 % your marksheet, 65 % your CAT. The marksheet half — the AR — is built from the same five things either way: Class 10, Class 12, your degree, full-time work experience, and gender. Maximum 38.

What changed is the other 65 %. The old rules took your raw score out of 204. The new rules take your percentile out of 100. That is the whole story, and here is why it matters: you can always score more marks, but you cannot score more than 100th percentile. Once you are past the qualifying line there is very little CAT left to win — only the gap between your category’s floor and 100 — while AR still spreads candidates across many points. So the marksheet quietly becomes the thing that decides it.

The scoring tables changed too, and far more quietly. Your degree used to be marked against one of five tables, picked by what you studied; Class 12 against one of three, picked by your stream. Now everything is marked against a single table. For most backgrounds that means the same marksheet is simply worth fewer points than it was last year. Nobody announced it as a cut, but that is what it is.

What this page is and is not

It compares the two sets of rules, using one made-up pair of candidates. Fill in both profiles and it works out how far apart each set of rules puts them, and what it would take to close that gap.

It is not a prediction of who gets in. Different years mean different papers and different people sitting them, and the new rules are already changing who bothers applying. It also stops at the shortlist: the written test and the interview together decide 60 % of the final call, and none of that is modelled here.

The new formula divides your AR by the average AR of the top 50 applicants. IIMA published 35 for 2024–26, 37 for 2025–27 and 38 for 2026–28, but not the 2027–29 figure. The 34–38 range puts one AR point between 1.417 and 1.584 percentile points, a 12 % band on every new-rules figure. The new table should push this number down: see chapter 5.

6 · Method, sources and what is assumed

Everything this page asserts, and the exact point at which it stops asserting.

The two formulas

New policy — PGP 2027–29, CAT-2026. Raw Composite Score = 0.35 × (AR ÷ top-50 AR average) + 0.65 × (CAT total percentile ÷ 100), then normalised by the average of the top 1 % of raw composite scores within the candidate’s undergraduate discipline. One AR point is therefore worth 0.35 ÷ 38 ÷ 0.0065 = 1.417 percentile points, everywhere on the scale. Within a discipline the normalisation divisor cancels, so it drops out of any same-discipline comparison — which is what this page measures. It does not cancel against the shortlist threshold, which is common across disciplines: you are not only beating a rival, you are clearing a line, and for that the divisor is decisive. IIMA has never published it.

Old policy — PGP 2026–28, CAT-2025. CS = (AR × 0.35 ÷ 38) + (overall CAT score × 0.65 ÷ 204). One AR point = 0.35 ÷ 38 × 204 ÷ 0.65 = 2.891 raw marks. Converting those marks to percentile uses the score-versus-percentile curve for the selected year, evaluated across the whole interval rather than by a local slope.

Rating tables

New. One common table for Class 10, Class 12 and bachelor’s: 1 point at or below 55 %, 2 above 55, 3 above 60, 5 above 70, 8 above 80, 10 above 90. Stream and discipline are ignored. Work experience pays nothing below 12 months, 0.20 per month from 12 to 36, and a flat 5 above 36. Non-male candidates score 3.

Old. Class 10 used the same table. Class 12 varied by stream and bachelor’s by one of five academic categories, each with its own band boundaries — AC-2 paid a full 10 above 63 %, where the new table pays 3. Those tables were re-verified against the April 2026 archived IIMA admissions page.

CAT curves

2022, 2023 and 2024 are inverted from the overall-score and overall-percentile columns of slot-wise score calculators, filtered for outliers on a rolling-median residual, then made monotone by pooled-adjacent-violators regression. Three corrupt rows in the CAT-2022 sheet were inflating that year’s 99th percentile by about 11 marks before the filter was added; the rejected-row count for each year is printed under the curve selector in chapter 2’s Advanced assumptions. The curves reproduce published benchmarks closely — 2024: 99th at 94.75 marks against the 95–97 published by IMS and Cracku; 2023: 99th at 76.0 against 76–78. No public dataset of actual CAT-2025 scorecards exists, so every CAT 2025 option here is an estimate. Cracku publishes a full table from the 55th to the 99.99th percentile, reproduced exactly and smoothed between anchors with shape-preserving (PCHIP) interpolation, which by construction cannot overshoot a published point. IMS publishes from the 80th to the 99.9th. Below each provider’s lowest anchor the curve borrows the 2023/24 proxy’s shape, so the bottom tail of both is illustrative rather than sourced. The default is the average of the two, on the view that where two independent estimates disagree the midpoint is a better guess than either, and the gap between them is the honest measure of how little is actually known.

Comparability

The two regimes are compared as mechanisms — the same hypothetical pair of candidates run through two formulas. They are not a counterfactual about outcomes. CAT-2025 and CAT-2026 are different papers sat by different pools, and the 2027–29 applicant pool is itself being reshaped by the rewrite. Read “the rewrite cost this profile two points” as a statement about the tables, not a prediction about shortlists.

Not modelled

Everything after shortlisting. The Final Composite Score is 0.50 PI + 0.10 AWT + 0.40 normalised CS under the new policy, against 0.50 PI + 0.10 AWT + 0.25 CAT + 0.15 AR under the old — so 60 % of the final decision is a room this page cannot see into. Sectional cut-offs, the old policy’s Stage-1 ACRC route with its C2 and C3 academic filters (both dropped in the new policy), and the old requirement of a positive raw score in every section are all checks rather than scores, so they gate the comparison rather than shift it.

Author’s note

I built this because a policy changed in the middle of an application cycle and nobody published the exchange rate. IIMA told applicants the weights — 35 and 65 — and left them to work out what that meant for a marksheet they could no longer change. Three findings survived every profile I ran through it, and none of them depend on whose profile it is.

One. The percentile ceiling inverts the balance of power. Above the qualifying floor there is a fixed, small amount of CAT left to win, and a plausible AR spread among qualifiers is larger than it. Under the old policy the reverse was true, because raw marks kept going up. Chapter 5 puts a number on it for your category.

Two. Collapsing five academic tables into one is a loss or a wash for nearly every background. CA, CS and CMA candidates lose up to seven points; medicine and arts degrees up to five; commerce and management two at 65–70 %, three at 70–80 % and two at 80–90 %; engineering three at 75–80 %. The only gain anywhere is a single point for an engineering, science or other degree between 55 and 65 %. This was never announced as a change in generosity, and arithmetically that is what it is.

Three. The unpublished top-50 AR average sets the exchange rate itself, so it moves every new-rules figure here by up to 12 %, including same-discipline comparisons. Anyone quoting a single exact number for what an AR point is worth in 2027–29, this page included, is quoting the midpoint of a range IIMA has not disclosed.

What I would take from it as an applicant: spend effort on the boundary that can still move, and stop grieving the one that closed years ago. Chapter 3 tells you which is which for your own profile, and the honest answer for most people is that the largest single number on the page is one they cannot move.
